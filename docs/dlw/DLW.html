#The Digital Logos Workstation

#Part One — The instrument

#1. Hearing it together

Human knowledge has never been more abundant, or more estranged from itself. The fields that know the most barely speak to one another. A physicist and a contemplative can spend their lives on the same question — what it is to be aware of anything at all — and never read a line of each other's work. The traditions that carried wisdom for thousands of years are shelved under religion, the sciences sit in their departments, and a person's own experience is filed under nothing at all. AI could help. Mostly it is being used to make more text, faster than anyone can read it.

Lay the fields side by side, though, and they are often saying the same thing in different keys. Kepler heard the planets as harmony and computed their orbits in the same breath. When this project worked through the structure of meaning carefully enough, it found the basic intervals of music already inside it. Knowledge has a musical shape: separate voices that can be heard together without being merged into one. What has been missing is an instrument for hearing them that way, and for playing them.

Music got its instrument. Making a record once took a studio, an engineer and someone willing to pay for the hours; Ableton Live put recording, arranging, mixing and performing into one window with one clock, and now someone with a laptop can make a record in their bedroom and have strangers dancing to it by the weekend. Live did not make music easy. It made the whole craft reachable, with controls you learn once and then play with everything you have.

The Digital Logos Workstation is that instrument for knowledge, and it is built on Live's own grammar. You can read a source and keep its thread to the last citation. You can set agents to build something and watch them work. You can give an idea a form you can see and hear, and build what you find into a world someone else can walk into. And because the person doing all this is also a world — with depths that never quite rise into words — the instrument holds both: the whole that can be understood, and the self who understands it.

It isn't easy. Working this way takes reading, patience and a willingness to be corrected by your sources. But when the voices start to sound together, there is nothing like it.

#2. Worlds

Everything in the DLW is a world, and every world is drawn as a body in space.

When you open it you see your own from close orbit: a planet turning slowly under its star, with the things you are living with gathered around it — the agents at work for you, the moments still open from today, the scenes you made last week, the people you have been talking to. Come down to the surface and the places on it are the things you are making. A project is a place on the ground, with paths worn between its landmarks; a book you are writing is another; a practice you keep to yourself is a room fogged to everyone but you. The day's work leaves tracks across the surface. Lighter things stay up in orbit, the files and context you have to hand right now, and drift off when you put them down.

Choose its seas and continents, how rugged the coasts run, how warm and wet the climate is, what colour the sky turns at evening, and reroll the seed if you want a new body from the same settings. Then place things on it and name them: a place, a palace you can walk into, a road between two of them, a portal through to someone else's world. Most of the building happens without your noticing. Every project you start becomes a place, and every walk you take wears a path.

The same holds in every kind of work. In Factory a task is a small world being made, its agents circling it and the helpers they spin up riding smaller orbits inside theirs, so that a whole programme of work hangs in front of you as a system you can take in at a glance. A Live set is a world, its tracks the bodies in it. You can go from your day to the work you commissioned to the song you are making and never learn a new way to look.

Earth is there too, the one world nobody built. It carries real geography, and anything in your own world can be anchored to it: a letter to the town it was posted from, an idea to the house where someone first had it, a walk to the coastline it followed. You cannot reshape Earth. You place things on it, as people always have.

A collaborator's planet turns nearby. A research group is a system of planets around a shared star, and a whole discipline is a field you can pull back far enough to see entire, with the relations between worlds drawn across the dark — one anchored to another, one taking part in another, one watching, a contribution crossing the gap like a particle, two fields joined at a remove. How far you look widens a step at a time: your world alone, your world with the projects and agents it holds, the shared field, the fields that field is joined to. Whatever lies just past the edge of your view is counted and offered.

What do others see? Only what you show them. You can look at your own world as yourself, as the field sees it, or as the public sees it, and whatever you have not shown lies under cloud; raising anything into wider view asks you first. Visit someone else's world and you land on what they have shown, tinted and clouded as they left it, with the agent that keeps it there to talk to. Being findable is not being reachable. Being reachable is not being in touch, and being in touch is not being trusted — each step is separate, and each is the owner's to give. If the other world's agent gives you something, it arrives in your world under their name, and stays theirs until you have made it your own.

So one person's work and everyone's knowledge turn out to be the same kind of thing. A private journal, a team's codebase, an archive of letters, a school of thought, the literature of a whole science: each is a world at its own distance, and you move between them by looking further out or closer in. The personal and the academic are near and far in one sky. You build your world by working in it, and as it grows it becomes something others can see, walk through and learn from.

Over every world there is a sky, and the sky belongs to the world. Its planets move as the real ones do; stand in today's, or in the sky of any day you choose. Two people under the same sky receive the same movements and answer them differently, because each of them is a sky as well, a whole system turning inside a person.

We are all, in a sense, aliens to one another.

#3. Three forms, three times

Anything in the DLW can be seen in three forms.

As a world, it has places, bodies and distances, and you can stand in it. As an expression, it becomes something you can see and hear: a scene of moving symbols, text and light, sounding as it moves. As a graph, it lays out what is connected to what, so that you can follow the threads and see the whole at once.

Each form can be taken in three times.

It can be live: the world as it stands right now, the agents moving around it, the field breathing as you work. It can be composed: taken into your hands and shaped — the walk ordered, the scene built, the graph arranged to make an argument. And it can be recorded: kept, played back, and so given to someone else.

A day of work shows all nine. As you work, your planet changes in front of you. In the evening you lay the day out as a graph and see what it touched. You take the moment that mattered into a scene and compose it until it says what you meant, then you record a walk through the places it came from. Next week someone else plays the walk and stands where you stood.

This is the instrument's language, and it is the same in every kind of work.

#4. Instruments

What you see in a pane is an instrument, and every instrument works in every kind of work. The kind of work decides what the instrument is looking at, never what it can do.

The world instrument shows the Earth, the solar system and every world in the field, at any distance: the whole field, a system, one body, a region, a single place. Switch it to its graph view and the same material lays out as connections instead of distances, coloured by what each thing is. This is the ground Technē's other instruments build on.

The canvas is a table to lay things out on: drag, group, frame, and draw a line between two things to say they are related. Where you put things is your own business. A line you draw is a suggestion until whoever owns that relation agrees to it.

The timeline lays material out over time: authored history beside the work that produced it, on one ruler that can stretch from deep time to this morning. Everything on it keeps the weight it actually has — fact, claim, inference, guess, dispute, or a reference nobody has found yet — and putting something next to something else never turns a coincidence into a cause.

The journey is an ordered walk through places, moments and things, with a pace, arrivals and a voice that tells it. You can play it, and you can record a new one from where you have just been.

The palace is a building you compose over one body of material, with rooms that each hold a part of it, a path through them, a private room for what is not for showing, and doors out to other places. It is how you give a whole subject a form someone else can enter.

Beside these sit the instruments any pane can hold for other kinds of material. The expression stage is the living field of Chapter 7. The knowledge graph is drawn for reading a wiki or a body of notes, link by link, with references nobody has resolved yet drawn as hollow ghosts, and with every node offering what its owner allows you to do with it. Documents, agent sessions and their threads, and a terminal are instruments too.

Open any of these in any pane, beside any other. Its controls appear in the device area at the bottom of the window whenever its pane has focus, and they are the full controls, wherever you are. The pane itself shows only the material.

An instrument can come in more than one build, the way Live has more than one reverb, each answering to the same controls. You arrange panes and instruments into sets, each kind of work has its default set, and you save your own.

#5. Central — the day's work

Central is where the day happens. It works like a code editor — files down the left, documents open side by side, a terminal a click away — except that the day itself is one of the documents. You write in it as you go: what you are working on, what you noticed, what went wrong. The work and the account of the work sit together, so at the end of the day nothing has to be pieced back together.

Documents come in four kinds. A Day has six faces, four for the work and two at its edges; you turn it face by face as the day moves, with the arrows where Live keeps its tempo. A Flow is a running conversation or journal with the people and agents named in it. Beings and Things are the people and objects your work is about, each tied to the world it belongs to, its sources, and the real place it stands.

An agent works beside you in whatever you have open. Select a paragraph and send it to the packet — the packet is exactly what the agent will read, and you can see it whole before you send. Press Play and the agent answers into the page, as a new entry, with its sources; you can follow each source back to where it came from. Press Record and talk, and your words go into the document. If the agent wants to change something, the change waits for you, unless you have armed it to write directly, in which case each change it makes comes with a receipt.

Look at Central through the world instrument and you see your planet. Your projects are places on it, today's work is a walk across it, and the files and context you are holding circle in orbit. Through the timeline you see today on one ruler: entries, runs, returns. Through the world's graph view you see what today touched, and through the knowledge graph how today's notes link into everything you already knew.

When the day ends it closes itself. What you learned is filed into your knowledge, what you made is kept, and tomorrow starts clean.

#6. Factory — worlds making worlds

In Factory you set agents to work, and what they make is worlds: a piece of software, a body of research, a place for others to stand. You say what you want in a sentence. The work breaks into tasks, agents take them up, and you watch it happen the way you would watch a workshop floor, stepping in where you are needed.

Agents sit on tracks, one session to a track, and their tasks are clips. Fire a task and it runs; fire a row and a batch of them runs together across sessions. A running task has no end drawn, because nobody knows how long it will take. A queued one has no length. A stalled one is striped and frozen where its output stopped, and the track it sits on lights a retry.

Turn to Arrangement view and every thread of work runs along one ruler, each with a needle that moves as its agent checks in. Below each thread its messages, files, terminal and web lookups run as lanes; click any one and it opens beside you.

Each agent's chain reads left to right as the path the work takes: in through a gateway, to the agent itself, through the rack of skills it may use, into the part of the world it may touch, and out through the place its work lands. You set how much it decides for itself, how hard it thinks, how many turns it has. Skills sit in the rack with a light each, on or off, and one knob can turn several of them at once. A group of agents is a rack of its own, one agent to a chain.

The transport becomes the foreman's desk. Play runs the work. Stop pauses at the next safe point and keeps what each agent had in mind; press it again to cancel. With Steer on, what you type reaches the agent mid-turn; with it off, it waits as the next task. Arm decides whether agents may write directly or must propose. Capture records what you do by hand, so an agent can learn it from you. Draw a window across the work and nothing is written before the plan is approved or after it ships.

Through the world instrument, a piece of work is a planet: the task is the world being made, its agents move around it, and their helpers ride smaller orbits inside theirs. A whole programme of work shows as a system of such worlds. Through the graph it shows as a map of what depends on what.

#7. Expressions — the living medium

Expressions is a medium you step into. Text, symbols, image, physics and sound share one field. Particles gather into the shapes you give them and move by forces you set. A plate under the field resonates, and as you sweep it the pattern on its surface sounds: the pitch rides the lines where the plate stands still, and each symbol can carry a small voice that sings its own frequency. Sound and image come from the same state, so when one changes the other changes with it.

You work in scenes. An Expression holds its scenes the way a Live track holds its clips, and you launch, loop, layer and record them the same way. Open a scene and you see its two sides together: the sequence of what happens, step by step, with the symbols in lanes like notes on a roll, and the sound it makes. Add a voice, pin a force, turn the plate on and sweep it until the figure says what you meant. Take the swell, layer the next section over it, capture the figure as a new scene, save.

The engine's controls sit in the device area as one studio: tools, the selected symbol's actions, formation, material, motion, the body of the field, the personal layer, capture. Behind the studio are the devices that govern the field itself — physics, pointer, relations between symbols, the medium they move through, contact, morph, focus, colour, ink, depth, resonance — each one whole, with every setting it needs. Put any of them on your rack as a small widget with its four most-played controls; open it full when you need the rest.

The transport turns musical in the literal sense. Tempo is the field's tempo. The time signature is the scene's ratio of hold to change. Key is the resonance the field is tuned to. Play plays the scenes. Arm the studio and Play records a take.

Agents can play this medium too. Through the Ta-Onta interface an agent can open an Expression, compose in it and perform it, so that what an agent finds can come back to you as something you see and hear, not only as text.

Expressions is not a separate world from your work. Anything in any mode can be given an expression — a day, a run, a source, a place — and every mode can open the expression instrument beside its own.

#8. Technē — crafting knowledge

Technē is where knowledge is made by building worlds. You find a subject and gather what surrounds it. You place it on the Earth where it happened and in the sky it happened under. You lay its history out on the ruler, walk its places in order, and build a palace to hold the whole. Here the six instruments are the material itself: places, peoples and agents, ways of seeing, and journeys through them.

Begin from the world. Your planet and the others turn around their star; pull in to a single body and its places are there, lit by the same sun, each one built from its owner's own settings. Build your own: choose its seas and continents, its climate and sky, then place locations on it and name them — a place, a palace, a portal to someone else's world, a route between. Earth's land is not yours to shape; you can only place things on it.

Every reference you bring in is real, from a real owner, at a known revision. When you trace a piece of material back — into its sources, its languages, the occasions it came from — the tracing is the work, and the way back stays open so you can trace it again.

Above the world the sky turns. Stand in today's sky, or in the sky of any date you choose, without losing your place in today; one tap brings you back to now.

A walk takes the history you have laid out and plays it. Each stop sets the clock to its year and the world to its place, and launches the scenes it carries — images, film, a live field sounding. A walk can go through several Expressions, one scene from each, or step down through the scenes of one. Record a walk and it lays itself out on the ruler, to be edited like anything else.

Under the instruments, Technē's transport walks. Tempo is the walk's pace, the time signature is which stop of how many, quantize is how a stop arrives. Key is how far you are looking and as whom: your world alone, or the shared field, or beyond; as yourself, as the field, as the public.

#9. Live — the studio it came from

Live is the music studio the DLW grew from, and it is kept whole: a set, its tracks, clips and scenes, its instruments and effects, rebuilt from close study of the original so that they behave as the original does. Make music here as you would in Live.

It is also the measure. Every other mode reads Live's grammar in its own way, and each reading can be checked against how Live itself behaves. A rack in Factory should behave like a rack in Live; a follow action in a walk like a follow action in a set.

Live shares the same worlds as everything else. A set is a world, its tracks are bodies in it, and a song can be walked stop by stop. In time Live will join Expressions, where sound and picture will share one timeline the way a film editor holds them.

#10. The hands

#10.1 Two views

Every kind of work has a Session view and an Arrangement view. Session view is a grid of launchable material, tracks across and rows down, for working out of order: trying, launching, combining. Arrangement view lays the same material on one ruler, for seeing the course of it. Tab switches between them.

#10.2 Tracks, clips, stops

A track is a stream of one kind of material, and in Live it plays one clip at a time. A clip is a piece of that material in a state: playing, stopped, queued, recording. In the DLW a clip is always the real thing, held at a known version — an entry in the day, a task, a scene, an event in history — never a copy.

The word scene always means an Expression's scene, which is a clip. What Live calls a scene, the row that launches one clip on every track at once, the DLW calls a stop: it stands several things together, the way a stop on a walk stands a place, a moment and the scenes it carries.

A follow action says what comes after a clip has played, and launch quantize says when a launch lands. In Factory they say which task runs next and whether your Stop lands now, after the current tool, or after the current turn. On a walk they say which stop follows and how it arrives.

Takes lie under a clip as they do in Live, and you choose the one that stands: the best capture of a scene, the attempt at a task that should land, the reading of a history you want to keep.

#10.3 Devices

Instruments make things: an agent answers, the field sounds, a world takes shape. Effects change what passes through them without replacing it: a highlight marks a passage and leaves it whole. They sit left to right in a chain, and the material passes through in that order.

A rack holds several chains side by side under one device: skills in an agent's rack, agents in a group, pads in a drum rack. A macro is one knob turning several settings at once, each within its range. Every device comes three ways: a small widget you can add to your rack, the whole device opened in the browser, and the device at full size.

When something changes a value, the value shows what changed it. An agent's edit carries the agent's name; a knob being moved by the time of day shows the time of day moving it. When you take hold of something an agent or instrument was moving, a light comes on to say so, and one press hands it back.

Anyone can build a new device that sits in the same chain, as Max for Live devices do in Live, and each of the suite's products brings its own: the day and its documents, the agents and what tunes them, the skills they carry, the work they do, the machines they run on, and the sky and its clock.

#10.4 The browser

The browser on the left holds what you can bring in: material to open, kinds of instrument to open in a pane, devices to add to a chain, and running things you can work with where they are. Preview, open and add are always three separate acts. Drag a skill onto an agent's chain and it is admitted; drag an agent onto a track and it takes the seat.

#10.6 Companions

Each kind of work has its companion in the panel on the right, with the tiles you want beside it: the packet, the trail of everywhere you have been, the agents, a terminal, results, the sky. In Central it is the agent working in your documents. In Factory it is a desk where results come back. In Expressions it is Nara, a presence that meets you in the form. In Technē it is Epii, who works beside the instruments and can tell you why each room of a palace is there. None of them judges your work for you.

#10.7 Going back

Everywhere you go is kept on a trail, and you can always go back: to the same place, the same page, the same point in the reading. Changing your kind of work never closes anything. Every place can be linked to and reopened.

#Part Two — The Game

#11. The figure

In 1943 Hermann Hesse published a novel about a game nobody had played. His narrator calls it "a mode of playing with the total contents and values of our culture" (The Glass Bead Game, Introduction), and says its player plays "like the organist on an organ."

Hesse never wrote the rules down. He wrote around them, closely enough that the game's parts can be counted: a fixed instrument; one language in which mathematics and music count equally; themes stated and worked out the way a fugue works out its subject; a silence after every symbol, kept so that the symbols would stay full; an archive that admits new symbols rarely, through one yearly competition; festival games that ran for days, and private ones that anyone could play; masters who taught, an order of players and the village they lived in; every game written down so it could be read back; and an ending. Hesse's Magister Ludi does not try to save the game. He knows a beautiful thing that has entered history will pass.

That is a description of a whole environment for meaning, made before anyone could build one. Live showed that a whole environment for a medium can be software: composition, sound design, arrangement, recording, mixing and performance had mostly lived in separate tools, and Live put them in one place with one clock. The DLW takes Live's proof and points it at what Hesse described: the contents of a world, played on one instrument.

The game grew out of music, and so does the DLW. Its first mode is a music studio, and every other mode plays on that studio's grammar. Hesse made music the model for a universal language. Here the universal language is built from a working musical instrument.

#12. The game's language

Hesse's players stated a theme and developed it. In the DLW the theme is any piece of a world, and the development is the movement between its three forms and its three times: a place in your world becomes a scene that sounds, the scene becomes a stop on a walk, the walk is recorded and played in someone else's world. That movement is the play. It is the same in every kind of work, because the instrument is the same.

Hesse distinguished two schools of the game. Formal games found their unity in the material. Psychological games found it in the silence after each move. The DLW keeps both: Technē works the material until it holds together, and Expressions lets you meet it as something lived.

Origin. Knecht spent years translating a single school game back, phrase by phrase, into mathematics, ornament, Chinese and Greek. The game's archive could always be read back into the disciplines it came from. Technē works the same way: every reference keeps its owner and its version through every reading, so that anything can be traced to where it came from. That tracing is a kind of care. It treats the origin as something still present, which is what Jean Gebser meant by the ever-present origin.

The silence after the symbol. For Hesse's audience the meditation was the heart of the game. In the DLW that place belongs to Nara, the presence you meet in the field: not a judge of your work, a listener and a reply. Whether the instrument should ever require a pause, as Hesse's game did, is still an open question, and nothing automatic fills the space until it is answered.

Teaching. Magister Ludi first meant simply schoolmaster, and Knecht ends as a teacher. Epii is the DLW's teacher: it works beside the instruments and explains, and you learn the instrument by playing it with Epii there.

The village. Hesse gave the game a village where its players lived. The DLW's shared field is that village with every player a world: you visit what others have shown, take back what they give you under their name, and show your own world as far as you choose. Hesse also kept the private game: "to this day everyone is free to play the Game privately." Your world, unshown, is where that game is played.

The score. Hesse's games were written down so they could be played again. Here a day closes into what it learned and made, a walk is recorded, a take is kept, and each record can be opened and played again by someone else.

Ending. Games end; festivals end; the score remains. A day in the DLW ends by itself. Whether a whole kind of work, or the instrument itself, could one day be retired with its record kept is a question the DLW keeps open, because an instrument that cannot imagine its end will defend it.

#13. Epi-Logos

The game the DLW exists to let anyone play is called Epi-Logos.

The name comes from a cycle of the word that runs from the silence before speech, through the first utterance, through speech between people, to a clear account; then to epi-logos, speech about speech, where language turns and sees itself; and from there to proportion and return. The project is named for that turn. An instrument that works on language from inside language, with care, is that turn made into something you can use.

Epi-Logos has two sides. One is a body of thought: an essay on the inwardness from which any world appears; Quaternal Logic, its formal language; Bimba, a map of the whole; the agent world of Ta-Onta; Nara's presence; Epii's depth; and the work that holds them together. The other is the system: the suite's products and the DLW itself, built so that they assume none of that thought and can carry anyone's.

The old word for the relation between the two is bimba and pratibimba: an original form and its reflection, the form made usable. The DLW is the reflection. Turn on the Epi-Logos lens, in any kind of work, and the body of thought lies over the system: your files become the corpus, Nara joins your companions, Nara's reading of the sky lies over Technē's, and what the instruments do with that material is the thought itself at work, not a picture of it.

The corpus is a game in its own right, themes taken up and set down over years. It is also the native game of the instrument. Anyone can download the DLW, make things with it, and show them in the shared field; that is how anyone plays.

Language sits under many of our conflicts, inner and outer, and we live in it before we learn to hold it. A language model is made of words carried on number, and so it can work on language from inside language, at scale. What it needs is a calling, and limits. In the DLW agents read, gather, propose, build and test; every change that matters passes through whoever owns what it would change, and a person has the last word. An agent can defer a decision. It cannot approve its own work into the canon.

#14. A day

The day opens. You stand on your own world, the field around you, the sky turning over. In Central you read, one tangent held open beside the main line. You mark three passages, note one, put all three in the packet and press Play. The agent answers into the page with its sources, and you follow one of them back to the text it came from.

The reading suggests a repair. You move to Factory and set it going, with a window drawn so nothing is written before you approve the plan. A small new world starts turning; agents work around it. When the changes and their checks come back, you read them and let them land.

Later you open yesterday's scene in Expressions. You add a third voice, pin its force, turn on the plate and sweep it until the figure says what the claim says. The new voice sounds its own pitch. You take the swell, layer the next section, capture the figure, and save.

Toward the close of the day you are in Technē. You gather what the day touched, lay it on the ruler, and record a walk across the places it passed through. You build a room for it in your palace and offer the whole back to your knowledge.

Before the day ends you visit another world. Its owner has shown some of it and clouded the rest. You open a conversation with the agent that keeps it, and it gives you one thing you did not have. You take it home under their name.

The day closes. What you learned is filed, what you made is kept, the walk is there for anyone you show it to.

#15. What the game warns

Hesse wrote the game's decline into its history, and three of his warnings apply to anything built in its image.

A form can grow more beautiful than what it carries, and then it is glass. The DLW meets that by playing the day in Chapter 14: whatever cannot do its part in that day is ornament.

A device that judges for the player — scores the scene, picks the reading — takes the silence away. The DLW's companions answer and explain; you decide.

Analysis can outlive the music it studied. The close study of Live serves an instrument that sounds, or it has lost its point.

The test of the whole is what people leave with: a day closed into what it taught, work landed, an expression shown, a reader who understood, a pupil who no longer needs the teacher. Hesse's novel ends with its Magister leaving the Order to teach a single pupil.

#16. Still to decide

  • What the units of time beyond the day are — a week, a season, a year — and how they close.
  • Whether Epii's teaching has a surface of its own or runs through every kind of work.
  • Whether, and where, the instrument should require the pause Hesse's game required.
  • Whether a kind of work, or the instrument itself, can be retired with its record kept.
  • Whether the cycle of the word maps onto the kinds of work at all.
  • How someone who is not the corpus's author enters the corpus itself, and what its discipline asks of them.
