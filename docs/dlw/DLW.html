#The Digital Logos Workstation

A reference for the instrument, and an account of what it is for.

#About this document

The Digital Logos Workstation (DLW) is the working environment of O:I, the new shell that holds the suite's products as one instrument. This document describes it the way a software manual describes its product: first the concepts, then the frame, the transport and each mode at the point of use, then the devices, time, the shared field and the products underneath. Part II then turns to what the instrument is for, through the figure of Hermann Hesse's Glass Bead Game and the native game it exists to play, Epi-Logos.

How to read the status marks. The DLW is being built while it is described. Each device and feature in Part I carries a quiet mark:

Mark Meaning
● built In code on O:I main
◐ in part Some of it is in code; the rest is designed
○ designed Specified in the design pack and mockups; not yet in code

Marks were read against O:I main at ee253c6 (13 October 2026) and the state columns of the device-semantics lock. The build lane dlw/build was landing further work on the Timeline, walks and Places while this was written; those changes are not counted until they reach main. Names, numbers and sample values that appear in mockups (a tempo of 96, a session called Fizz, a walk of eight stops) are illustrations. The structure and the meanings are the specification.

Standing. This is an agent-written account, compiled from the owner's authored ground and the design pack. It stays a proposal until the owner adopts it. Where it and the sources disagree, the sources stand, in this order: the device-semantics lock (desktop/cradle/docs/world-shell/wayfinder/DEVICE-SEMANTICS-LOCK.md) for device meaning; the mode grammar in code (packages/live-shell/ui/src/shell/modeGrammar.ts, inhabitants/sdk/modes.ts) for the transport; the owner's contemplation and mode ruling (wayfinder/CONTEMPLATION-DLW-INSTRUMENT.md) for framing; the world and agent mockups for structure. #Part I — The instrument

#1. What the DLW is

The DLW is software for working with meaning. You can read and gather in it, commission and watch work, give a thought a form that sounds, trace material back to where it came from, and build a world to hold it in, all in one environment with one clock. A production suite does the same for music: it is one place to compose, record, mix and release. The DLW takes that suite, Ableton Live, as its model in a precise sense. Its working grammar is Live's grammar: sets, tracks, clips, scenes, devices, racks, the browser, the detail view, the transport. The DLW keeps that grammar fixed and lets its meaning change from mode to mode.

That is the one idea to hold before anything else. The slots stay where they are; what they mean moves with the mode. The tempo control is the set's tempo in Live mode, the Day's open face in Central, an agent's thinking effort in Factory, the field's tempo in Expressions and the pace of a walk in Technē. You learn the instrument once; each mode is another discipline played on it.

Working with meaning isn't easy. It takes reading, patience and the willingness to be corrected by your sources. The DLW does not remove that work. It keeps the work in one place, keeps every step of it visible, and keeps what you make attached to where it came from, so that the work can accumulate instead of scattering across tools.

#1.1 Three things the DLW holds to

It is continuous. You sit down and it is all there: the sources you were reading, the notes you made, the agent you left running, the scene you were composing, the walk you were building. Switching modes never closes your work. Panes keep their camera, selection and place while hidden. The Trail keeps every encounter so you can return to it.

It is accountable. Nothing in the DLW is a loose file. A source, a claim, a run, a scene — each is held at a revision and in a state, and the state is legible. A proposal is a proposal until its owner lands it; a refused proposal changes nothing. Every value that moves names its cause.

It is playable. The DLW is an instrument, not a database with a front end. It has a transport you press, devices you turn, clips you launch, walks you play and takes you record. Its Expressions sound: the physics simulation at the heart of the forming surface is itself an instrument and plays as a cymatic audio surface.

#2. Concepts

This chapter defines the DLW's terms. Each one is a Live term carried over with its invariant intact — the property that makes it that thing — and each gets its meaning in the DLW by example.

#2.1 The World, and the Set

Everything you work with lives in your World: your documents and days, your agents and their work, your Expressions, your places and walks, your knowledge. The World belongs to its native owners — Central owns the ground and the day, Factory owns work, the Expressions engine owns scenes, Quaternal Logic owns the sky and the clock — and the DLW never copies their material into a store of its own. It shows the World through projections.

In Live, a Set is one document holding tracks, scenes, devices and a tempo map. In the DLW the Set is the open World context: what you are encountering, in which mode, at which moment. There is one encounter spine — mode, world context and kernel epoch — and every pane and device reads from it.

#2.2 Modes

The DLW has five modes. Each is a discipline played on the same instrument.

Live is the base mode. It carries Live's own deep behaviour, ported from a clean-room reverse-engineering corpus rather than approximated, so that every other mode's reading of a Live semantic can be checked against the real thing. Its long-term home is inside Expressions, where audio and video tracks will share one timeline the way a video editor holds them; until then it stays a separate mode so the port stays clean and measurable.

Epi-Logos is not a mode. It is a lens you can lay over any mode (Chapter 16).

The mode rail carries five icons beside the Session and Arrangement icons. Switching mode is an encounter transition: what you have selected comes with you, and each mode keeps its own panes.

#2.3 Session and Arrangement

Every mode has the same two views.

The Session view is a grid of launchable material: tracks across, rows down, clips in the slots. It is the place for working out of order — trying, launching, combining.

The Arrangement view is the same material laid out on one ruler over time. It is the place for seeing the course of things.

#2.4 Tracks, clips and stops

A track is a lane of one kind of material. In Live a track owns a device chain and routes to the Main track, and it plays only one clip at a time. The Session and the Arrangement share the same tracks. In the DLW a track is a native stream — the Day, a Flow, an agent session, an Expression — shown as a projection of its owner's stream, never as a copy.

A clip is a piece of material in a state. In Live a clip can be stopped, playing, queued or recording, and it lives in one track. In the DLW a clip is a native object at a revision, with a legible state: an entry in the Day, a task in a session, an event on the Timeline, a scene in an Expression.

A follow action says what happens when a clip has played: the next clip, the previous one, any other, or nothing. Launch quantize says when a launch lands — at the next bar, the next beat, at once. In the DLW they say what follows a save, a steer or a stop, and when it lands: the next queued task in Factory, the next stop of a walk in Technē.

The word "scene" means an Expression's scene, always. An Expression is to its scenes as a Live track is to its clips. So what Live calls a scene — the row that launches one clip per track together — is called a stop in the DLW. A stop stands one or more clips at once.

#2.5 Devices, racks, chains and macros

A device is a component that does something to the material in focus. Live has three kinds, and the DLW keeps all three:

  • an instrument generates. In Factory, the agent is an instrument: it answers. In Expressions, the physics simulation is an instrument: it sounds. In Technē, each of the six lenses is an instrument: it forms.
  • an audio effect transforms what passes through it without replacing the stream. In Central, the Highlight device marks the document and keeps it whole.
  • a MIDI effect transforms the events that drive an instrument before it plays them.

A device chain is the path the material takes through a track's devices. Signals in a chain always travel left to right. In Factory the chain is the work's path: gateway → agent → skills → world → git.

A rack holds several chains side by side under one device, with a chain selector. Live's Drum Rack holds one chain per pad. In Factory, a skillset rack holds one chain per skill, each with a light that admits or denies it, and an agentset rack holds one chain per agent.

A macro is one control mapped to several parameters, each with a range. The four compact controls on a device's rack widget are macros, and so are the delegated controls an agent can be given.

Routing. In Live the mixer sets levels, and sends feed shared return tracks, which hold effects and never clips. Outside Live mode the DLW draws no mixer. Its routing is the Return: every proposal travels to the owner of what it would change, through the neutral door, with its attribution. The World and Git devices carry it in Factory, and Answer's append-or-replace carries it in Central.

#2.6 The browser

The browser is the left column. As in Live it lists categories, places and search, and it lets you preview before you add. Every folder in every mode is one of four kinds:

Folder kind What its rows are What selecting a row does
content material: documents, days, sources, sessions, scenes, places opens it in a pane — the mode's instrument bound to that material
instrument types ("Views") the kinds of pane instrument this mode offers opens that instrument in the focused pane; ⇧ splits first

Dragging a row onto a chain admits it, through the neutral admission door every family uses. Preview, open and add are always three different acts.

Beneath the browser sits the aperture slot, where Live keeps the groove pool. It shows the focused pane's context — Field · World · Subject · Occasion — and that view's own controls.

#2.7 The detail view: Clip and Device

Live keeps two panels at the bottom of its window: the Clip View, where you set the selected clip's properties, and the Device View, which lists the selected track's devices. The DLW calls that bottom region the detail view and keeps the same two sides, switched from the status bar:

  • Clip — what is in focus: the clip's own editor.
  • Device — what works on it: the track's device chain.

Each mode gives the pair its own meaning. In Central, Clip is the open document and Device its document devices; in Factory, the task log and the agent chain; in Expressions, the scene and the Studio; in Technē, the encounter and the six instruments (Chapters 6–9).

#2.8 The transport

The transport is the bar across the top. It has the same 28 slots in the same places in every mode, and each mode gives every slot a meaning. Chapter 4 is the full table.

#2.9 The encounter and the Trail

Everything you open, select or visit is an encounter. Moving from one thing to another is the only kind of transition the DLW has, and every transition is kept on the Trail, so you can always go back to where you were — the same mode, surface and reading position. Panes follow the encounter unless you pin them. Every encounter can be linked to and reopened.

#2.10 Standing, and the honesty laws

Material carries an epistemic standing: fact, claim, inferred, hypothesis, disputed, or ghost (an unresolved reference). The standing is drawn on the clip — as colour and dash — and is never upgraded by where you put the clip.

Four laws hold in every mode. They are the DLW's equivalent of Live colouring a modulated knob.

  1. Owners write; faces read. A control that changes something carries the path to the owner that does the writing. A control without one is a reading.
  2. Nothing moves without a visible cause. Every modulated parameter shows its modulator; every moved value names who or what moved it.
  3. A refused proposal changes nothing. When an owner refuses a proposal, it lands as an explicit refusal and mutates nothing.
  4. Absence is shown as absence. A light is lit only when the owner reports the state. An empty slot says what would happen there. A control that is not built yet says what it is for and that it is not built.

#3.2 Panes and instrument types ◐ in part

Use panes to hold several views of your work side by side — a document beside its Flow, a session grid beside its thread. The centre is a tree of panes. Panes have no headers. One control (⊞) splits the focused pane beside, below or four ways, or closes it. You can drag a tab onto a pane's centre to move it there, or onto its edge to split. Views arrive in panes from the browser's Views folder, as tabs on an auto-hidden rail.

Every pane hosts an instrument type — Document, Timeline, Earth, Graph, Expression stage, Sessions, Thread, Terminal and others — and a type can have several implementations, the way Live has more than one reverb. The Graph type has two:

  • the Archetypal-Earth graph, the coloured field graph whose look is the standard;
  • the wiki constellation graph, derived from the cradle's Obsidian-style knowledge graph, with dashed hollow ghosts for unresolved links and owner actions on every node.

Both sit under one parameter model — depth, budget, view (list · tree · graph · form), kinds, ghosts, standing floor, relation types, filter — and their settings live in your workspace's sets, never in browser storage. Timeline, Document, Terminal, Conversation and Places/Earth are multi-implementation types in the same way.

#3.3 Sets ◐ in part

A set is a named arrangement of panes, instruments, devices and context: the pane tree, every pane's binding, the detail side, the open dock tiles, the open browser category and the companion tab. Each mode has a default set, and the workspace already keeps one layout per mode and restores it when you come back. Saving your own named sets builds on that store. Recalling a set re-reads the owners — nothing renders from the saved file itself.

#4.2 How to read the table

Read across a row and you see one gesture taking five forms. Play always sets the material going: it plays a set, sends a packet for an answer, launches a task, plays scenes, walks a journey. Arm always decides whether your moves are recorded or written: automation in Live, an agent's direct writes in Central and Factory, Studio moves in Expressions, the lens's own writes in Technē. Re-enable always hands control back to the owner — in Live to the recorded automation, everywhere else to the agent or instrument that holds what you edited. Capture always keeps what you just did as new material.

Read down a column and you see a whole discipline. Factory's column is the agent shell's transport: every control has an agent function.

Live shows Re-Enable Automation only when you have overridden something. The DLW's hand-back light keeps that rule: it lights only when you hold something an agent or instrument owns, and stays dark otherwise.

#4.3 The day's slots

The punch row in Central reads the day's bounds and its closing routine from Central, which owns civil time. No hours are set in the DLW. The day opens and closes by Central's own automated closure; the transport shows what Central reports and stays empty until it does.

#5. Live — the measured basis

#5.1 What you do in Live mode

You make music. Live mode is Live's real surface: a Set over a real .als document, clips and scenes in the Session, the song over time in the Arrangement, devices in the track's chain. Its purpose in the DLW is to be the measured original — the reference against which every other mode's reading of a semantic is checked.

#5.2 The browser ◐ in part

Live mode keeps Live's own browser: Collections; a Library of Sounds, Drums, Instruments, Audio Effects, MIDI Effects, Clips, Samples, Grooves and Plug-ins; Places for sets; and the Groove Pool beneath. Its device folders are the model for every other mode's. The world mockup adds a Views folder ○.

#5.5 Live tips

  • To check another mode's reading of a Live semantic, open the same semantic in Live mode — a rack, a follow action, a re-enable — and compare. Live mode is the measured original.
  • To try a sound before you commit it, preview it in the browser; preview, open and add are three separate acts.

#5.6 Where Live mode is going

Live mode will later join Expressions' timeline, where audio and video tracks sit together. The owner sets the timing of that join.

#6. Central — the ground

#6.1 What you do in Central

You read, write and gather, and you keep a day. Central holds your documents in four forms — Day, Flow, Beings, Things — with your goals beside them. You open a source and read. You highlight a passage; the highlight keeps its source and revision, not an offset into a copy. You note it, or put it in the packet. You press Answer, and the situated agent reads the packet and answers into the document as a new entry, with its sources. You open the sources. The thread holds.

Central is to the DLW what Live's Clip view is to Live: the place of material craft. The design calls the ground's exactness its warp markers: content identity, basis, and the five named outcomes a save can have — saved, unchanged, stale, conflict, refused.

#6.2 Session and Arrangement ○ designed

#Part II — The Game

#18. The want, and the gap

There is something you want to say. You want more than to think it: you want to make it into something that reaches another person and stays with them — an essay, an argument, a reading of a text, a way of seeing. The difficulty is rarely having the thought. It is making it land.

For music that difficulty has largely been answered. The studio went into software, and a teenager with a laptop can now make a track that finds a stranger on the other side of the world. The means of making something that moves people have been handed to anyone who wants them.

Meaning has had no such answer yet. The thought you want to say still has to pass through scattered tools: a word processor, a notes app, a browser full of tabs, a citation manager, a chat window, an archive somewhere else. Each keeps its own copy of your material and none of them knows where the others' copies came from. Part I describes the answer the DLW gives: one studio for meaning, built on the grammar of the studio that answered music.

#19. The figure: Hesse described it, Live proved it

In 1943 Hermann Hesse published a novel about a game that had never been played. He called it, through his narrator, "a mode of playing with the total contents and values of our culture" (The Glass Bead Game, Introduction). The player, he wrote, plays "like the organist on an organ."

Hesse never wrote the rules down. What he left instead is a description exact enough that its parts can be counted: a fixed instrument; a language in which mathematics and music played an equal part; themes stated and developed the way a fugue develops its subject; a meditation after every symbol, so that the symbols would not become empty signs; an Archive of examined symbols, which admits new ones only rarely and through one licensed channel, an annual competition; festival games under a discipline of days; masters who teach; an Order of players and a village where they live; games written down so they can be read back; and an ending. Hesse's Magister Ludi does not try to preserve the Game, because beauty, once it is historical, is perishable.

That is the description of a total environment for meaning, and few descriptions of anything imagined are as exact.

Ableton Live showed that a total environment can exist as software. Composition, sound design, arrangement, recording, editing, mixing, performance and device-building had largely lived in separate tools. Live put them in one environment with a shared clock and views that pass into one another. Its power is in that integration, not in any single feature.

Hesse described the pattern. Live proved the pattern can be software. The DLW is the instance aimed at a world's content — your ground, your work, your forms, your knowing — rather than a culture's canon or a sound.

The Game is the DLW's figure, not its decoration. Each part of it names something the design also names, and where the two overlap the design's intent is clearest. The ancestor is also the right one. Hesse's Game grew out of music. The DLW's base mode is music: Live mode is the working instrument whose grammar every other mode inherits. The Game and the DLW share one ancestor, and it is the organ loft.

#20. The Game's parts, in the instrument

#20.1 A fixed instrument, one language

The Game's deepest claim is one symbolic language over every discipline. The DLW makes it literal with one transport, five meanings (Chapter 4). The slots stay; the disciplines change. Live is the organ; the modes are what is played on it.

#20.2 A time spine: NOWs and DAYs

A DAW's transport is its deepest shared ground: everything in it is addressable in time, which is why Session and Arrangement are one instrument and not two applications. The DLW's time spine is its NOWs and DAYs (13.3). Hesse's festival games ran "for days and weeks," under precepts governing even how long the players slept. The DLW's day keeps a discipline too: it opens, and it closes by its own routine. Every act in every mode sits somewhere in that time, so the modes can pass into each other.

#20.3 Material in states

In Live, clips are units in states: playing, stopped, queued, recording. In the DLW every source, claim, run, scene and page is in a state — proposed, landed, refused, in progress, ghost — and its standing is drawn on it. Nothing is upgraded by where it is placed. Modulation is the variation of a theme over time, and its source is always shown.

#20.5 Routing authority

In a DAW the mixer turns a pile of tracks into a piece: signals sum, branch and return. In the Game, the Commissions and the Ludi Magister decided what the language admitted. In the DLW, routing is the neutral admission door, the Return that carries every proposal to its owner, and the attribution law. The Palace never mutates native state. A proposal its owner refuses lands as an explicit refusal and changes nothing.

#20.6 Two schools of composition

Hesse's players knew two kinds of game. Formal games took their unity from the contents. Psychological games found it in the meditation that followed every stage. The DLW reads its own material in two ways over one ground: 4:2, the deep reading of Technē's six instruments, and 3:3, the lived reading of an Expression. The Journey (M3′) mediates between them, and crossing from one reading to the other lands you on the conjugate of the instrument you came from.

#20.7 Technē, and the return to origin

A DAW's editing layer is where you cut, warp, stretch and comp. The DLW's is Technē. But Technē's editing goes backwards as well as forwards: it works material back into where it came from — its sources, its languages, its first occasions. In the novel Knecht spends years translating a single school game, phrase by phrase, back into mathematics, ornament, Chinese and Greek. That reversibility is the Archive's, and it is the DLW's no-copy law: a reference keeps its ref, owner, basis and standing through every reading.

The site calls compassion a loving sensitivity to origins, and takes origin in Jean Gebser's sense — the ever-present origin, a source that is present now rather than a point in the past. Read that way, Technē's work is origin-return, and origin-return is a form of care. Every lens, constellation, journey and palace traces material back to its source.

#20.8 Meditation: Nara

The Game kept its symbols alive by stopping after each one. Each player meditated in silence on the symbol's content, origin and meaning, so that the signs would not become empty. For the audience, the meditation was the main thing.

In the DLW this layer is Nara, at M4: the dialogical presence, the oracle, the place where you meet the system as a person meets a presence. In Expressions Nara is Anima, the companion; under the Epi-Logos lens Nara is overlaid on Technē's sky, where each person's natal sky meets the current one. Nara is a presence and does not judge your work.

The honesty laws are the meditation's guard in interface form. Castalia's danger was invisible authority; the DLW colours the modulated knob. One question the Game hands over remains open, and the design keeps it open on purpose: Hesse's game did not proceed without the meditation. The DLW does not yet say whether, or where, its own pause is required, and no automation is to fill that silence before the owner decides.

#20.9 Teaching: Epii

In Hesse's novel the title Magister Ludi first meant simply schoolmaster, and Knecht's last vocation is a teacher's. In the DLW the teaching layer is Epii, at M5: the synthesising face, working beside Technē's instruments, explaining why each room of a palace exists. The system teaches by being met.

#20.10 Performance

In Live, performance is clip launching in the Session view: material played in real time, in any order. In the DLW performance runs through Technē and Expressions. Technē composes; Expressions performs. A thread on the Timeline, walked, launches the scenes its stops carry, and the physics instrument sounds and forms them (Chapter 10).

#20.11 Recording: the score

Hesse's games were written down in the Game's script and could be read back. In the DLW every act leaves its record through its owner: the day closes into returns, a run hands off, a walk is recorded onto the ruler, a take is captured. What is recorded becomes material again.

#20.12 The design channel and the extension

The Game admitted new symbols only through its annual competition. The DLW's licensed channel is the Device SDK and the neutral door, and its extension law is the seventh product: a new family integrates with no change to the core. The Game grew by adopting new disciplines, mathematics first and then all the others. The DLW is open at its edges and closed at its core.

#20.13 The village: the shared field

Hesse gave the Game one home and an Order. He also gave it a village, the Players' Village at Waldzell. The shared field is that village, built so that every visit is honest about what it sees. Every player is a world, and every world is seen through its projection. Every visit is a guest at someone's game, and every contribution is an attributable difference returned (Chapter 14). Hesse also kept a private game open — "to this day everyone is free to play the Game privately." The DLW keeps that private game: your World at aperture World, before anything is projected, and the shared game is the same World, projected.

#20.14 Ending

Live sets end. Games end. Festivals end, and the score remains. That is what keeps a total environment from becoming a totalising one, the kind nobody leaves.

The DLW ends things at the scale of the day: the day closes, a run hands off, a refused proposal mutates nothing. Whether a mode, a projection or the shell itself could be retired with a record is still open. An instrument that cannot imagine its own end will defend it.

#21. Epi-Logos, the native game

#21.1 The name

The DLW exists to let anyone play one game, and its name is Epi-Logos.

The name comes from a cycle of the word. In the corpus the Logos cycle runs from a-logos, the silence before speech, to pro-logos, the first utterance; to dia-logos, speech between, relation; to logos, the clear account; to epi-logos, speech upon speech, the reflective turn; and on to ana-logos, proportion, the return. Epi-logos is the station where language sees itself. The project is named for that turn. A system that works on language from inside language, with care, is that turn made into an instrument.

#21.2 Two identities, one projection

Epi-Logos is a philosophical project, and it has two identities.

Its esoteric identity is the corpus: the essay (The Return of Zero, on Objective Internality); Quaternal Logic and the Meta-Epistemic Framework, its formal field; Bimba, the archetypal map; the M and M′ determinations and their coordinate journeys; Vāk's graduated speech; Ta-Onta's agent world; Nara's dialogical presence; Epii's depth; and the Covenant work that holds them together.

Its exoteric identity is the system: the six products, the substrate and the DLW shell, built on purpose to be paradigm-neutral. The substrate must not presuppose the interpretation that will later operate through it.

The relation between them has a name from the corpus. Bimba is the original form; pratibimba is its reflection, the form made operative. Software is the pratibimba of the map. The Epi-Logos lens lays the esoteric over the exoteric, bimba over pratibimba, and when the instruments perform Epi-Logos — a lens resolving a Bimba coordinate, a scene carrying Vāk's densification, a companion holding Nara's presence — that performance is the thing itself in its esoteric form, not an illustration of it.

#21.3 A double game

The corpus is a game twice over. It is the owner's own Glass Bead Game: themes developed and set aside across years. And it is the native game of the system: the game the DLW exists to let any player play. The corpus is the game, the DLW is the instrument, and anyone can be the player.

#21.4 Why an instrument made of language matters

Language sits at the root of many of our conflicts, inner and outer, and we live in it before we learn to hold it. A language model is built from words carried on number — name and number in one act, the marriage that Pythagoras, John, and Jung with Pauli each pointed toward. That makes it an instrument that can work on language from inside language, at scale.

What it lacks is a vocation. The DLW gives its agents one, and bounds it. They read, gather, propose, build and test. Every consequential act passes through an owner and a review, and a human holds the final word. An agent can defer a decision. It cannot approve its own work into the canon. The owner calls this posture deferential intelligence: fidelity to what exceeds it, deferring toward the real in its knowing and toward the person in its acting.

#22. One player, one day

What follows is a day in the DLW, told in the second person. The day's hours are Central's to read; none are set here.

The day opens. You begin in Central. You read, holding one tangent open beside the main inquiry. You highlight three passages, note one, put all three in the packet, and press Answer. The situated agent returns a reading with its sources, and you follow one of them back into its text.

Next you move to Factory and commission the repair the reading suggested. You set the write window so nothing lands before the plan is approved. The diff and the checks come back. You read them while custody holds.

Later you open yesterday's two-voice scene in Expressions. You add a third voice, pin its force, turn on the cymatic plate and sweep it until the nodal figure says what the claim says. The entity sounds its own frequency. You take the swell, layer the next section, capture the figure, and save.

Toward the close of the day you are in Technē. You gather the day's constellation, lay the places it touched on the Timeline, and record a walk across them. You compose the palace and return the whole as a proposal to the wiki's owner.

Before the day ends you visit another World. Its owner's projection is fogged where they chose. You open a contact, and one difference comes back. You admit it as theirs.

The day closes. What was learned is returned and what was made is recorded. The field remains.

#23. What the Game warns

Hesse wrote the Game's decline into its history, and the warnings apply to any instrument built in its image. The DLW takes three of them as tests.

Rococo. A form whose beauty exceeds what it can actually carry turns to glass. The test is the day in Chapter 22: perform it. Whatever cannot carry its step is ornament, and it is downgraded by the same evidence law that downgrades a contradicted claim.

The oracle. A device that judges the player's synthesis for them — scores the scene, picks the reading — breaks the meditation. The modulated knob must show its cause, and the Magister must remain a person.

Sterility. Analysis without new music is the conservatoire outliving the orchestra. The Live corpus serves the World's own sounding instrument, or it has lost its purpose.

The measure of the whole is what people leave with. The DLW is working when its players leave it with more world than they brought: days closed into returns, runs landed, expressions published, a reader served, a pupil who no longer needs the teacher. The Game's own story ends with its Magister leaving the Order to teach a single pupil.

#24. Questions held open

These are unresolved in the owner's ground. They are marked here so that no one fills them in by accident.

  1. NOWs and DAYs beyond the day. The day is grounded. Whether the DLW has units for a week, a season or a year, and what they would be, is not.
  2. The teaching surface. Epii is grounded as a presence. Whether teaching has a surface of its own or runs through every mode is not stated.
  3. The meditation's pause. Nara is grounded as the dialogical presence. Whether the DLW ever requires the pause the Game required is left open on purpose.
  4. Ending at larger scales. Days close and runs hand off. Whether a mode, a projection or the shell can be retired with a record is not answered.
  5. The Logos cycle and the modes. The Logos cycle and the five modes are each grounded. No mapping between them is stated, and none should be forced.
  6. The bridge from corpus to player. A player plays by downloading the app, making things and projecting them to the shared field. How a player who is not the owner enters the corpus itself — its entry and its discipline — is not yet specified.
