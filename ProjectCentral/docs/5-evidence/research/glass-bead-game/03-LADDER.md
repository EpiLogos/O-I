# 03 — The ladder to be left behind

The owner's phrase. This lane documents what that ladder-character consists
of: in the Order's own law, in Knecht's arc, in Hesse's poems, and in the
older lineage of the image — with the Hesse–Wittgenstein link status checked
rather than assumed.

## 1. The Order legislates its own instrument as a thing to be outgrown

At Knecht's admission to the Order, the Music Master assigns him, "as the
subject of a meditation exercise… the familiar passage":

> "If the high Authority appoints you to an office, know this: every step
> upward on the ladder of offices is not a step into freedom but into
> bondage. The higher the office, the tighter the bondage. The greater the
> power of the office, the stricter the service. The stronger the
> personality, the less self-will." (ch. "In Office")

The speaker then immediately resigns his own office — "I am about to resign
my office; my release has already been granted" — modelling renunciation at
every rank. The ladder is in the constitution before it is in the biography.

## 2. Knecht's chain of departures

- **The Designori episode happens inside Castalia** (correction to a common
  misreading: there is no "year outside"). Plinio, an outside "hospitant" at
  Waldzell, is the worldly counter-principle; their debates are "a double
  concerto" of world and Mind. Plinio supplies the novel's own
  anti-escapism line: to remain in Castalia "would mean escaping. It would
  be a fine, a noble escape perhaps, but still an escape." Knecht's actual
  stays "outside" in his free years are at the Benedictine abbey Mariafels.
  A student notebook from this period already shrinks the Game to a part:
  "The whole of both physical and mental life is a dynamic phenomenon, of
  which the Glass Bead Game basically comprehends only the aesthetic side."
- **Refusing the ambition-track.** The biographer: Knecht "had neither tried
  for his high honor nor consciously adapted himself to it. It had come
  almost against his will." He declines the mission to Hirsland and the
  offer of a university appointment outside: "Appointment at a university
  would from the start mean my fitting into a traditional, sanctified, and
  mechanized bureaucracy, and what I crave is just the opposite of that."
- **The resignation.** The rules permit any member to resign, but "for
  decades no one had ever tested the relevant clauses"; Master Alexander
  calls the step "something unheard of in living memory," incomprehensible
  though "not illegal in any formal way" (ch. 12). Knecht is, in practice,
  the first Magister Ludi ever to return the seals. His own account: the
  idea first came "a few months after my appointment," reading his
  predecessor's dread of the annual public Game.
- **The Circular Letter's core** (quoted fully in 02-EVOLUTION-DECAY §3):
  the refusal "to prevent or postpone the ultimate end of our Game," and the
  turn on the title itself — "although we have almost forgotten it,
  'Magister Ludi' of course originally meant not the office we have in mind
  when we use the word, but simply schoolmaster." Hence the petition: "I
  herewith request the Board to relieve me of my office as Magister Ludi and
  entrust to me an ordinary school, large or small, outside in the country."
  The Board declines to accept any of it; he departs regardless.
- **The explicit ladder-passage**, in the biographer's final chapter:

> "The same strict, clear, unequivocal, straight path that had brought him
> to Waldzell, to Mariafels, into the Order, into the office of Magister
> Ludi, was now leading him out again. What had been a consequence of acts
> of awakening had likewise been a consequence of partings. **Castalia, the
> Game, the magistracy — each had been a theme which needed to be developed
> and dismissed; each had been a space to pass through, to transcend.**"

The metaphor chosen is the Game's own: each stage of the life was a *theme*,
developed and dismissed — the fugue-figure applied to a biography. It is
followed by the memory of the student poem "Transcend!" ("Überstieg"), whose
imperative title says the doctrine in one word.

- **The last evening.** Knecht copies a Rückert stanza — "Our days are
  precious but we gladly see them going / If in their place we find a thing
  more precious growing… **A child whom we are teaching, a booklet we are
  writing**" — and says: "I am a teacher, and am on the way to my task, to
  the child I mean to teach." The Magister Ludi's final act of play is to
  become what the title always meant: a schoolmaster.

## 3. "Stufen"

The novel's appendix of student poems includes "Stufen" ("Steps/Stages,"
dated 4 May 1941), the ladder-doctrine in lyric form. Verified lines:

> Wie jede Blüte welkt und jede Jugend / Dem Alter weicht, blüht jede
> Lebensstufe, / Blüht jede Weisheit auch und jede Tugend / Zu ihrer Zeit
> und darf nicht ewig dauern.
>
> Es muss das Herz bei jedem Lebensrufe / Bereit zum Abschied sein und
> Neubeginne…
>
> Und jedem Anfang wohnt ein Zauber inne, / Der uns beschützt und der uns
> hilft, zu leben.
>
> Wir sollen heiter Raum um Raum durchschreiten, / An keinem wie an einer
> Heimat hängen…
>
> … Wohlan denn, Herz, nimm Abschied und gesunde!

In English paraphrase: each life-stage flowers "to its time and may not
endure forever"; at every call of life the heart must be "ready for farewell
and new beginning"; "in every beginning dwells a magic" that protects and
helps us live; we should "cheerfully pass through room after room, clinging
to none as a homeland"; "Take leave, then, heart, and heal!" The doctrine:
clinging to a mastered stage brings "Erschlaffen" (slackening); only the one
ready for departure leaves a bounded space "without penalty." Death itself
is framed as possibly one more sending "into new, young spaces."

(The wording queried in the brief — "Herz, sei aufbrechend bereit" — is not
the poem's text; the verified formulations are the ones above. The claim
that "Stufen" opens some editions of the novel is UNVERIFIED; it stands in
the appendix cycle of Knecht's student poems. The alleged poem
"Geronton-Geron" is unattested in any source and is almost certainly a
misremembering.)

## 4. The older lineage of the image

**(a) Wittgenstein, Tractatus 6.54.** Exact wording (Pears translation):

> "My propositions serve as elucidations in the following way: anyone who
> understands me eventually recognizes them as nonsensical, when he has used
> them — as steps — to climb beyond them. **(He must, so to speak, throw
> away the ladder after he has climbed up it.)** He must transcend these
> propositions, and then he will see the world aright."

**Link status: parallel, not influence.** No scholarly evidence that Hesse
read the Tractatus; the documented sources of the Game are Nicholas of
Cusa's *De ludo globi*, Leibniz's universal characteristic, Josef Matthias
Hauer's music theory, chess, and Frazer's *Golden Bough*. The comparison
exists as scholarship on structural coincidence (Georgina Edwards,
"Language Games in the Ivory Tower," 2019, PhilPapers). The parallel is
nonetheless exact enough to name: an instrument whose mastery consists in
recognizing it as a stage and stepping beyond it.

**(b) The Buddha's raft.** Alagaddūpama Sutta (MN 22): "I have taught the
Dhamma compared to a raft, for the purpose of crossing over, not for the
purpose of holding onto" (Bhikkhu Bodhi's rendering: "for the purpose of
crossing over, not for the purpose of grasping"). Having crossed, one leaves
the raft on the bank and walks on. The Hesse connection is lifelong and
documented — *Siddhartha* (1922), the Indian Life, Adrian Hsia's account of
Hesse's movement "from Buddhism to Confucianism." The raft is the ferry
variant of the ladder: the instrument's success is disembarkation.

**(c) Vasudeva the ferryman.** In *Siddhartha*, the ferryman teaches "not
through words — he teaches through the river," and tells Siddhartha he will
reach the goal "but not from me." Siddhartha's awakening comes hearing "Om"
above the river's many voices, after which Vasudeva departs into the
forest — his crossing complete. Ziolkowski's foreword reads *Siddhartha*'s
hero as an earlier instance of the same three-stage progression the Game
novel completes.

**(d) The finger and the moon.** Śūraṅgama Sūtra, fascicle 2: "If a man
points at the moon with his finger, the other should look at the moon
following the finger; if he takes the finger for the moon, he loses not
only the moon but the finger too." The Game's symbols — and Castalia's
offices — are fingers. The novel's "humorless readers" who took the Game
literally are finger-watchers. (Direct Hesse–Zen scholarship: none found;
treat as an analog, not a documented lineage.)

**(e) Via negativa / Gelassenheit.** Scholarship connecting Hesse's
renunciation theme to Eckhart's letting-go exists but is thin and indirect
(Nic Craith, *Religions* 15(3):307, 2024, discusses *Gelassenheit* through
Eckhart and Heidegger alongside Hesse; Albrecht Classen, 2021, places
*Magister Ludi* in a monastic-intellectual lineage reaching back to
Eckhart). Canonical instead is Knecht's own word for the pattern:
**awakening** — never revelation. "I never thought of those awakenings as
manifestations of a god or daimon or of some absolute truth. What gives
these experiences their weight… is… their reality." Epiphanies that
rearrange rather than complete, each one costing a departure.

## 5. The ending as ladder-completion — and the book's silence about it

Three structural facts, all verified:

1. **The full German title is an epitaph**: *Das Glasperlenspiel. Versuch
   einer Lebensbeschreibung des Magister Ludi Josef Knecht samt Knechts
   hinterlassenen Schriften* — "…together with Knecht's *posthumous*
   writings." The frame knows he died.
2. **The official biography refuses the ending**: it "leaves off before the
   final sections of the book, remarking that the end of the story is beyond
   the scope of his biography," and the last chapter, "The Legend," is
   "reportedly from a different biography." The Game cannot narrate its own
   transcending; the account of the ladder's toppling exists only as legend.
3. **The three Lives close the loop**: each life — mastery, failure or
   futility of the achieved form, death as passage — rehearses "a progress
   from step to step, a series of realms to be traversed and left behind"
   (the passage Timothy Leary, of all people, quoted accurately before
   missing its next move).

Knecht's death is rendered as struggle and gift at once: "he was fighting
for the boy's respect and comradeship, for his soul — when he was already
fighting with Death, who had thrown him and was now holding him in a
wrestler's grip." Tito's final thought makes the ladder's purpose explicit:
the guilt "would utterly change him and his life, and would demand much
greater things of him than he had ever before demanded of himself." The
ladder's product is not the climber's arrival but the person left standing
at the bottom of the thrown-away ladder, changed.

Ziolkowski's one-sentence form of the reading: "**The spiritual ideal, once
attained, has now been put back into the service of life.**" And: Knecht
remains true to his name — *Knecht*, servant.

## 6. Misreadings to avoid

- **Leary's Castalia.** Timothy Leary named his Millbrook foundation
  "Castalia" — "Our psychedelic celebrations… are very much like the bead
  game" (*The Politics of Ecstasy*, 1968, verified pp. 189–192, 295). He
  quotes Knecht's awakening-passage and stops at ecstatic
  self-transcendence, skipping the next move: service to a concrete other,
  and the Order's rule that "the higher the office, the tighter the
  bondage." Ziolkowski's mockery: the Game is "purely a symbol of the human
  imagination and emphatically not a patentable 'Monopoly' of the mind" —
  aimed at readers who claimed to have "invented" it.
- **The escapism reading** is foreclosed inside the novel: Plinio's "a noble
  escape perhaps, but still an escape"; the frame of a posthumous history
  of a fallen culture; Hesse's insistence (to Staiger, Faesi, Pannwitz)
  against mistaking the utopia for a norm.
- **The Castalia-as-ideal reading** ignores that the novel's structure is
  "the fulfillment of a long sought ideal" *and* "its ultimate rejection"
  (Ziolkowski) — and that the biography drops the pen before the ending.
- **Title folklore.** *Magister Ludi* is in the German subtitle from the
  start (Fretz & Wasmuth, 1943); the report that Hesse preferred the plain
  title *The Glass Bead Game* and found "Magister Ludi" misleading is
  reported but not documented in a primary letter (UNVERIFIED). The pun does
  the work either way: *ludus* is both game and school — the instrument's
  true name was always the schoolroom.
